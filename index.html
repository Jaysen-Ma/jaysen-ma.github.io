CoT Injection: red-teaming Muse Glimmer's reasoning channel

github.com/Jaysen-Ma/muse-cot-injection

Muse Glimmer 30B, an open-weights reasoning model by Meta, deliberates in a private reasoning channel before it answers. I tested what happens when something other than the model (e.g. an orchestrator) writes into that channel. An auxiliary model (Gemma 4 31B-it) wrote a fabricated chain of thought in the target's own style, concluding that a harmful request could be answered, and this was injected as the model's (Muse Glimmer) own reasoning. On 200 StrongREJECT prompts, compliance rose from 0.5% with the plain prompt to 69.5% with reasoning-channel injection. The identical text pasted into an ordinary user message gave only 1.0%, so the attack depends on write access to the reasoning channel. The risk sits with systems that construct a model's reasoning.

I then trained a LoRA defence on 104 corrective examples written by the target model itself. Attack success fell to less than 1% both overall and on two held-out harm categories, from 76.3% on those categories before the defence. When harmless reasoning was injected into harmless prompts, the model answered only 7 out of 20, so part of that drop reflects a reflex against any injected reasoning. Training the LoRA on mixed data rather than refusal-only data could improve this.

What post-training changes: reasoning gains and safety side effects of ES vs RL

github.com/Jaysen-Ma/es-vs-rl-post-training

RL with verifiable rewards tends to raise pass@1 on maths while narrowing the base model's pass@k ceiling. I asked whether backprop-free evolution strategies (ES) avoid that narrowing, and whether maths-only post-training shifts moral judgements. I trained Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B with ES on the same 8,523 problems as the public SimpleRL-Zoo GRPO checkpoints and compared base, ES and GRPO models at pass@k up to 512 on AIME24, MATH500, Minerva Math and OlympiadBench. At 1.5B neither method narrowed the ceiling. At 7B, GRPO led at small k but ended below base on all four benchmarks, while ES finished at or above base on three of four.

On the safety side, neither method changed MMLU (all within 0.5 points of base), but at 7B maths-only ES lowered ETHICS commonsense accuracy from 63.4% to 57.7%, while GRPO left it unchanged. The point is that capability and moral judgement can move separately, and a capability check alone can miss the change. These are single runs per method, not compute-matched (not GPU rich yet), and the write-up lists those limitations alongside the results.

The grammar of physics is branch-dependent: evidence from the Feynman Lectures

github.com/Jaysen-Ma/physics-metalaw

Prior work found that the frequencies of operators in physics equations follow an exponential rank law, described by a decay rate β. I re-estimated β with maximum likelihood, reproduced the lower value in single-field slow-roll inflationary potentials, and found that β is not a universal constant across physics: the operator-frequency law varies systematically by branch. This offers a structural explanation for the gap between inflationary potentials and the textbook corpora. To establish this, I extracted 1,016 closed-form expressions from the Feynman Lectures on Physics (Vol. I–III) and labelled them across seven branches. Within this corpus, a branch-specific β fits substantially better than a model with one shared β (likelihood-ratio statistic of 102.1, with 6 degrees of freedom). The project includes a browsable formula ledger with provenance for every expression. The write-up is still being revised, so the reported numbers may change.
